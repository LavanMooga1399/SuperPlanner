Universal Planning & Optimization Platform — Product Blueprint
1. The product vision
You should build a Universal Planning and Optimization Engine: a platform that accepts a goal, a
collection of tasks, resources, deadlines, dependencies, constraints, and changing conditions, then
generates an optimized, executable schedule.
It should work for manufacturing production planning, UPSC preparation, project execution,
workforce scheduling, supply-chain planning, machine allocation, personal productivity, and other
scheduling problems without requiring a completely new application for every use case.
The key architectural decision is this:
Do not build one giant Genetic Algorithm that tries to solve every problem. Build a configurable
planning platform with a shared scheduling core, a domain-neutral problem model,
interchangeable optimization algorithms, and domain-specific adapters.
That distinction will determine whether the product remains useful as new use cases are introduced.
Your HBL Production Planning Genetic Algorithm work is a useful starting point. You have already
worked with capacity, demand, inventory, efficiency, production constraints, and comparing planning
scenarios. Those concepts belong in the manufacturing adapter, while the reusable optimization
engine should be independent of manufacturing-specific assumptions.
Manufacturing planning
Allocate jobs to machines, respect material availability, setup times, capacity, due
dates, maintenance windows, and production priorities.
Learning and exam preparation
Convert a syllabus into topics, prerequisites, study sessions, revision cycles, mock
tests, and a deadline-aware study plan.
Complex project planning
Coordinate interdependent tasks, teams, deliverables, milestones, parallel
workstreams, budgets, and uncertain durations.
Real-time operational scheduling
React to machine failures, urgent orders, resource unavailability, delays, changing
priorities, and newly arriving work without unnecessarily rebuilding the entire
schedule.
The platform should make these different problems feel like variations of the same workflow:
Describe → Structure → Validate → Optimize → Simulate → Approve → Execute → Learn → Replan.
2. Product principles
These are the principles I would use to guide the entire build.
1. Configuration over hardcoding: New planning domains should be introduced through schemas,
constraints, objective definitions, templates, and plugins wherever possible.
2. Feasibility before optimality: Never present a schedule that violates a mandatory constraint as a
valid plan simply because its fitness score is high.
3. Multiple algorithms: Use Genetic Algorithms where they are effective, but allow constraint
programming, mathematical optimization, graph algorithms, heuristics, and hybrid solvers.
4. Human control: Users must be able to lock tasks, override assignments, protect critical deadlines,
compare alternatives, and approve a schedule.
5. Incremental replanning: When reality changes, preserve as much of the approved plan as
possible while repairing what has become infeasible.
6. Explainability: Show why a task was placed at a particular time, why a deadline may be missed,
which constraints are binding, and what trade-offs drove the result.
7. Reproducibility: Store input versions, algorithm versions, configuration, random seeds where
relevant, and solver results.
8. Domain-specific UX, shared engine: A factory planner and a student should not see identical
screens, even though they use the same scheduling infrastructure.
One important limitation: no finite, universal optimizer can guarantee the mathematically best
solution for every arbitrary problem. Your goal is to create a universal framework that selects suitable
representations and solvers, produces feasible plans when possible, and makes the quality of its
results measurable.

The most important work is not writing the GA. It is designing a model expressive enough to

Start-to-finish B cannot finish until A starts

optimization model. Use solver-specific variables and a common schedule-result format.
5. Constraint engine: make rules configurable
The constraint engine is what turns a generic task list into a real scheduling problem.
5.1 Hard and soft constraints
Hard constraints
Conditions that a valid schedule must satisfy. Examples include no double-booking a machine,
respecting task dependencies, staying within mandatory resource capacity, and honoring fixed
events.
Soft constraints
Preferences that may be violated when necessary, with a measurable penalty. Examples
include preferring morning study sessions, reducing machine changeovers, minimizing
overtime, or avoiding team context switching.
Never make the optimizer treat every rule as a weighted penalty. A sufficiently large collection of
attractive soft objectives must not compensate for violating a mandatory safety or capacity rule.
A reliable optimization pipeline should be:
1. Validate input structure.
2. Detect obvious infeasibility.
3. Construct an initial feasible schedule, when possible.
4. Optimize among feasible schedules.
5. Independently validate the result.
6. Return the plan with explicit feasibility and quality status.
5.2 Constraint registry
Each constraint implementation should declare:
Unique identifier and version.
Scope: task, resource, interval, dependency, project or global.
Hard/soft classification.
Configuration schema.
Validation method.
Solver-specific compilation method, if supported.
Independent evaluation method.
Explanation and remediation guidance.

5.3 Infeasibility explanations
This is a major product differentiator.
Instead of returning only No feasible solution , explain problems in terms users understand:
The deadline requires 18 hours of work, but only 12 hours of eligible capacity remain.
Two mandatory tasks require the same exclusive resource during overlapping periods.
A predecessor task cannot finish before its successor's latest permitted start.
A machine maintenance window conflicts with every eligible slot for a job.
Where supported, the engine should return a minimal or near-minimal conflict set and identify
candidate relaxations. The interface should distinguish between proven infeasibility, detected
conflicts, and a solver that simply timed out without finding a feasible solution.
6. Optimization algorithms: use the right tool for each problem
This is the technical heart of the product.
6.1 Algorithm portfolio
1. Genetic Algorithm (GA)
Core capability
Useful for complex mixed decisions, sequencing, resource assignment and multi-
objective search. It is especially attractive when a valid schedule can be represented
as a chromosome and repaired or decoded efficiently.
Python options: DEAP or pymoo; custom operators for domain-specific scheduling.
2. Constraint programming / CP-SAT
Excellent for discrete scheduling with precedence, optional activities, alternative
resources, no-overlap and cumulative capacity constraints.
Python: Google OR-Tools CP-SAT.
3. Mixed-integer optimization (MILP)
Useful for linear costs, assignment, production quantities, inventory balance,
overtime, and planning problems with mathematical structure.
Python: HiGHS or SciPy interfaces; commercial solvers can be optional later.
4. Local search and metaheuristics
Simulated annealing, tabu search, iterated local search and large-neighborhood
search are useful for improving a valid schedule through targeted changes.
Implement a shared neighborhood interface and reuse the feasibility checker.
5. Graph algorithms and heuristics
Topological sorting, critical-path analysis, earliest/latest start calculations, priority
rules, greedy assignment and list scheduling provide fast baselines and useful initial
solutions.
6. Simulation and robust optimization
Model uncertain durations, demand, breakdowns, availability and other risks.
Compare candidate schedules over multiple scenarios instead of optimizing only for
an ideal forecast.
These are complementary tools, not competing choices where one must win universally.
6.2 Solver selection strategy
Build a solver-selection layer rather than letting a user manually select an algorithm for every
problem.
The selector should consider:
Number of tasks and decision variables.
Dependency graph structure.
Number and type of resources.
Time discretization.
Linear versus nonlinear objectives.
Hard-constraint complexity.
Required response time.
Whether an initial schedule exists.
Whether the user needs a proven optimum or a good feasible plan.
Available compute budget and solver licensing.
Initially, implement transparent, rule-based selection. Learn from benchmark data later; do not start
by building a machine-learning model to choose algorithms.
6.3 How the Genetic Algorithm should work
Use a hybrid, feasibility-aware GA.
Chromosome design
Avoid assuming that each chromosome must directly contain absolute start times for every task. That
representation is often brittle because small mutations can break precedence, capacity, and calendar
constraints.
For general scheduling, consider a priority-based indirect representation:
A priority/order gene for eligible activities.
Resource-choice genes for activities with alternative resources.
Optional mode or batch-choice genes.
Optional scheduling preference genes.
A schedule decoder converts these decisions into concrete start/end times and allocations while
respecting hard constraints wherever the representation permits it.
This is often more robust than treating every timestamp as an independent gene.
Initial population
Create a diverse population using several methods:
Earliest-deadline-first.
Critical-path priority.
Shortest-processing-time and longest-processing-time heuristics.
Resource-aware greedy scheduling.
Randomized topological orders.
Existing approved schedules with controlled variation.
Hybrid solver-generated schedules.
Do not initialize the entire population randomly. Random schedules can be mostly infeasible,
especially with many dependencies and scarce resources.
Selection, crossover and mutation
Use tournament selection or another configurable selection method.
Use precedence-preserving crossover when chromosomes encode task sequences.
Use resource reassignment and order perturbation as mutation operators.
Use repair operators to resolve conflicts after genetic operations.
Preserve a small elite group to avoid losing strong solutions.
Monitor population diversity and stagnation.
Increase exploration when the population converges prematurely.
Your previous HBL experiments also highlighted why disabling mutation is risky: it removes a source
of new genetic material and can lead to premature convergence. A nonzero, adaptive mutation
strategy is a better starting point than turning mutation off.
Fitness evaluation
Use a multi-objective evaluation model with a clear feasibility hierarchy.
A candidate schedule might be scored on:
This is an illustration; every problem should activate only the objectives relevant to its domain.
Use a feasibility-first ranking:
1. Feasible candidates rank ahead of infeasible candidates.
2. Infeasible candidates can be ranked by violation severity during search.
3. Feasible candidates are ranked by Pareto dominance or an explicit user-configured objective.
4. The final selected schedule is independently validated.
For multi-objective search, NSGA-II is a reasonable starting point. It can return a Pareto frontier,
allowing users to compare faster, cheaper or more balanced schedules. Do not collapse every
objective into a single arbitrary weighted sum unless the user explicitly chooses that trade-off.
Stopping criteria
Terminate when one or more configured conditions are met:
Maximum runtime.
Maximum generations or iterations.
No material improvement for a defined number of generations.
An acceptable objective threshold is reached.
The compute budget is exhausted.
Always return the termination reason and whether optimality was proven, estimated, or not
established.
7. Hybrid optimization: the preferred production architecture
A pure GA is not necessarily the best way to solve every scheduling problem. A hybrid engine will
usually be more practical.

A practical sequence for a difficult planning problem is:
1. Run fast graph analysis to determine precedence and lower bounds.
2. Generate one or more baseline schedules using heuristics.
3. Use CP-SAT or MILP when the model fits their strengths.
4. Run GA or another metaheuristic for difficult mixed decisions.
5. Improve the strongest candidates with local search or large-neighborhood search.
6. Validate the result independently.
7. Return several useful alternatives, with their trade-offs and feasibility status.
Do not always run every algorithm. A tiny planning problem should not require an expensive
optimization pipeline. A large or difficult problem should be allowed to consume more compute
when the user asks for a better result.

acceleration only if measured workloads justify it.

9.2 Versioning rules
This is essential for trustworthy optimization.
Every solver run should reference:
An immutable problem version.
An immutable scenario version.
The solver and algorithm version.
The complete effective solver configuration.
A random seed where applicable.
The code/build version.
The start/end times and termination reason.
The resulting schedule version and validation report.
Editing the source problem while a solver is running must not silently change that run's inputs.
Use optimistic concurrency control for edits, such as a version number or ETag. When a user approves
a candidate schedule, verify that its inputs are still compatible with the current planning state.
For production-grade auditability, retain an explicit distinction between:
1. A candidate schedule.
2. An approved schedule.
3. The active schedule used for execution.
4. Historical schedules retained for comparison.
Do not automatically overwrite the approved schedule every time the optimizer finds a better
candidate.
9.3 Data storage rules
Store task dependencies relationally.
Use JSONB for flexible domain metadata and versioned configuration, not as a replacement for

Store very large solver traces, simulation results, exports and population snapshots in object

Keep aggregate metrics in PostgreSQL for dashboard queries.
Index by tenant/workspace, problem version, run status, task identifiers and relevant scheduling

13. Real-time planning and automatic replanning
Real-time scheduling is a different problem from generating a schedule once. The platform must
respond to events while protecting work that is already underway or explicitly committed.
Examples:
A machine fails at 10:30 AM.
A production order becomes urgent.
An employee calls in sick.
A student misses three planned study sessions.
A project deliverable is delayed by an external approval.
A new task arrives with a deadline earlier than the current plan can accommodate.
13.1 Event-driven architecture
Event processing rules
Every event should include an event ID, tenant/workspace, affected entity, occurrence timestamp,
receipt timestamp, source and payload version.
Use idempotency to prevent duplicate event processing. Persist events before acknowledging them
where reliable delivery matters, and design recovery around the possibility that a worker crashes
between processing and publishing.
For events that arrive late or out of order, use event timestamps and the current authoritative
planning state to determine whether the event is still applicable.

14. Simulation, uncertainty and scenario planning
A plan that is optimal under perfect assumptions may be poor in the real world. Your product should
eventually assess whether a schedule remains useful when estimates are wrong or resources become
unavailable.
14.1 Scenario engine
Allow users to clone a baseline scenario and modify assumptions without changing the original.
Example scenarios:
Normal demand.
High demand.
Reduced staffing.
Machine breakdown.
Delayed material delivery.
Less study time available.
Project approval taking longer than expected.
Each scenario should reference its parent scenario, the changed assumptions and the resulting
schedule.
14.2 Monte Carlo simulation
Where task durations or resource availability are uncertain, generate multiple plausible realizations.
For a project, sample task durations from calibrated distributions, calculate the resulting schedule or
execution policy, and estimate:
Probability of finishing by the deadline.
Expected completion time.
P50 and P90 completion time.
Expected operating cost.
Resource bottleneck frequency.
Probability of violating service levels.
Do not assume all task durations are independent. Shared causes, such as weather, supplier delays or
a common overloaded team, can create correlated outcomes.
14.3 Robust optimization
For high-value planning problems, support risk-aware objectives such as:
Minimize expected cost.
Minimize a high-percentile completion time.
Minimize the probability of missing a deadline.
Minimize worst-case cost within a defined uncertainty set.
Balance expected performance against risk.
The user should see the assumptions and uncertainty ranges behind these results. Simulation
estimates are not guarantees, and their accuracy depends on the quality of the input distributions.

19. Testing strategy: prove the planner is trustworthy
The most dangerous failure is a schedule that looks good in the UI but violates a hard constraint.
Testing must therefore cover the mathematical engine, application behavior and operational reliability
independently.
19.1 Optimization test suite
Test category What to verify
Schema validation Invalid durations, unknown resources and malformed dependencies are rejected
Dependency tests All precedence relationships and lags are respected
Calendar tests Tasks fit availability windows, shifts and holidays
Capacity tests No resource exceeds its capacity in any relevant interval
Feasibility tests Infeasible problems are identified or reported with honest status
Algorithm tests Solvers produce valid results for supported problem classes

19.2 Independent schedule validator
Build a validator that does not depend on the GA decoder or the solver's own feasibility claims.
For each resulting schedule, independently check:
1. Every required task is present exactly as expected.
2. Start and end times are valid.
3. Durations and allocated effort are consistent.
4. Precedence and lag constraints are satisfied.
5. Resource capacities are respected.
6. Calendars and availability are respected.
7. Assignments are eligible for each task.
8. Materials, budgets and other modeled constraints are satisfied.
9. Fixed, completed and locked activities remain protected.
10. All objective metrics are calculated correctly.
Use property-based testing to generate random small problems and verify general invariants.
For small instances, compare the engine's results with a solver that can prove optimality. This is
particularly valuable for finding bugs in the GA decoder, constraint compilation and objective
calculations.
19.3 Benchmark suite
Create a versioned benchmark dataset with:
Simple precedence-only problems.
Resource-constrained project scheduling.
Job-shop and flexible job-shop scheduling.
Workforce shifts.
Inventory and production planning.
Multi-objective mixed scheduling.
Dynamic event-driven replanning.
Infeasible and nearly infeasible problems.
Record feasibility rate, time to first feasible solution, objective quality, runtime, memory and
disruption under replanning.
Compare the GA against greedy baselines, CP-SAT or MILP where applicable, and hybrid strategies.
Use multiple random seeds for stochastic algorithms. A single lucky run is not sufficient evidence of
superiority.

23. The five mistakes I would avoid
1. Making the GA the entire product. The GA should be one component of the optimization platform.
The data model, feasibility checker, schedule decoder and validation engine are equally important.
2. Treating every objective as a weighted sum. Hard feasibility must be separated from optimization
preferences. Use Pareto optimization where users genuinely have competing objectives.
3. Building a completely generic UI. Share components and data structures, but use domain-specific
terminology, templates and workflows. A student should not have to understand machine setup
times, and a factory planner should not have to configure revision cycles.
4. Reoptimizing everything whenever something changes. Support locked decisions, freeze
horizons, local repair and schedule-disruption penalties. A theoretically better plan can be
operationally worse if it disrupts too many commitments.
5. Scaling before benchmarking. Do not start with Kubernetes, distributed GAs or GPU acceleration
simply because the product is an optimization platform. Establish representative workloads, identify
bottlenecks and scale the expensive components independently.
24. My final recommendation: what to build first
Build these components in this order:
1. Canonical planning model — the common language for tasks, resources, dependencies,
calendars, constraints and objectives.
2. Independent feasibility validator — the foundation for trustworthy schedules.
3. Graph analysis and baseline scheduler — establish a fast, understandable reference
implementation.
4. CP-SAT integration — solve supported discrete scheduling problems and establish a strong
comparison baseline.
5. Hybrid Genetic Algorithm — add a decoder, feasibility-aware genetic operators, adaptive
mutation, repair and multi-objective search.
6. Solver orchestration and durable jobs — manage asynchronous runs, cancellation, progress,
versioning and resource limits.
7. React Planning Studio — build the task editor, dependency graph, Gantt chart, resource views
and scenario comparison.
8. Three domain adapters — manufacturing, exam preparation and project management. Use them
to test whether the abstraction really works.
9. Live replanning and simulation — add them once baseline planning is correct and measurable.
The central architectural principle is that the domain model describes the problem, the constraint
engine describes what is allowed, the objective model describes what is desirable, and the solver
searches for a schedule. Those responsibilities should remain separate.
That separation is what will let you evolve your manufacturing-planning experience into a general-
purpose optimization platform rather than another application tied to one industry.
My suggested first engineering deliverable is a technical design specification that defines the
canonical data model, solver interfaces, constraint semantics, GA chromosome/decoder design, API
contracts and benchmark suite in enough detail that implementation can proceed module by module
without major architectural ambiguity.

5. The AI should explain its recommendations
The most valuable feature is not simply showing an allocation. It is explaining why that allocation fits
the user's circumstances and what could make it unsuitable.
For every proposed plan, provide:
Recommended action: Amount, frequency, destination and expected holding period.
Reasoning: How the recommendation supports the user's stated goals and constraints.
Assumptions: Expected returns, inflation, taxes, fees and other inputs used in calculations.
Alternatives: More conservative, balanced and growth-oriented scenarios where suitable.
Trade-offs: Potential return, downside risk, liquidity and missed-goal risk.
Confidence and limitations: Which conclusions are based on deterministic calculations and
which depend on uncertain forecasts.
Replanning triggers: What changes should cause the plan to be reviewed.
For example, the platform could explain that increasing monthly contributions may improve the
probability of meeting a goal, while choosing a higher-risk allocation may increase both upside and
downside. It should not describe uncertain returns as guaranteed outcomes.

For an India-focused launch, the financial module needs its own data governance and compliance
layer.
Use verified, timestamped product information, including applicable interest rates, fees, maturity

Separate user-provided assumptions from sourced market data and calculated results.

Keep recommendations, user approvals and material changes auditable.
Clearly distinguish financial education, goal-planning calculations, product comparisons and
personalized investment advice.
There is an important product-boundary decision here. In India, personalized investment advice and
certain product-distribution activities may trigger SEBI or other regulatory requirements depending
on how the service operates. Before offering actionable personalized investment recommendations
commercially, obtain specialist legal and compliance advice on the applicable registration, disclosure,
suitability and distribution obligations. Do not assume that calling the system an AI planner exempts

4. Generalize the product around a universal problem definition
Now let us define the architecture without assuming the problem is necessarily a timetable, a
manufacturing schedule, a project plan or a financial portfolio.
The most useful abstraction is a decision problem evolving through time under constraints and
uncertainty.
A universal planning problem can be represented as:
Where:
: Goals and desired outcomes.
: Decisions the system is allowed to make.
: Resources and their limits.
: Mandatory rules and preferences.
: Objectives and trade-offs.
: Uncertainty and assumptions.
: Time horizon and temporal rules.
: Events and observed changes.
This representation is deliberately broader than a task scheduler.
For example:
Manufacturing decisions include production sequence, machine assignment and batch quantity.
Exam preparation decisions include study-session allocation, topic order and revision frequency.
Financial decisions include contributions, allocations, cash reserves and rebalancing.
Logistics decisions include vehicle assignments, routes and delivery timing.
Business planning decisions include budget allocation, project prioritization and staffing.
Not every problem has tasks or timestamps. A portfolio allocation may be primarily a mathematical
allocation problem; a vehicle-routing problem may require routes and geographic constraints; a
manufacturing problem may combine continuous quantities with discrete sequencing.
Therefore, the canonical model must support different decision structures—not force every
problem into a universal task table.

Why a problem compiler matters
The canonical model should describe the user's problem in a stable, domain-independent form. The
compiler then transforms it into a representation suitable for the selected solver.
For example, a capacity rule may become:
A cumulative scheduling constraint in CP-SAT.
A linear inequality in a MILP model.
A penalty or repair rule in a GA.
A state-transition rule in a simulation.
Not every rule can be translated into every solver. The compiler must declare what is supported, what
was approximated and what could not be enforced.
This is a more robust design than implementing a universal constraint class that pretends every
algorithm can evaluate every rule identically.

5. Generalize constraints and objectives properly
5.1 Constraint categories
Build a typed constraint system with more than just hard and soft labels.
Hard constraints: Must be satisfied.
Soft constraints: Preferences that can be traded off.
Conditional constraints: Apply only when a condition holds.
Temporal constraints: Describe timing, precedence or permitted windows.
Resource constraints: Describe availability, capacity and consumption.
Policy constraints: Encode rules specific to an organization or user.
Risk constraints: Limit exposure to specified adverse outcomes.
Robustness constraints: Require acceptable performance across a set of scenarios.
Dynamic constraints: Change when the observed state changes.
These categories should be metadata and modeling concepts, not a single inheritance tree that
makes every constraint implement every method.
For example, a financial liquidity rule and a manufacturing capacity rule both restrict available
resources, but their mathematics and evaluation semantics differ.
5.2 Objective registry
Define objectives as independently testable metrics with explicit units and direction.
Examples:
Minimize completion time.
Minimize cost.
Minimize deadline violations.
Maximize throughput.
Minimize schedule disruption.
Maximize expected goal attainment.
Minimize downside risk.
Maximize resource utilization within acceptable limits.
Minimize environmental impact.
Maximize quality or service level.
The objective registry should include:
Identifier and version.
Measurement units.
Minimize/maximize direction.
Aggregation method.
Applicable problem classes.
Required data.
Whether the metric is deterministic or estimated.
Any normalization used for comparisons.
Never compare two schedules by blindly adding minutes, rupees, probability and utilization
percentages. The system needs a defined preference model or Pareto analysis.
5.3 Preference learning without giving up user control
Over time, the platform could learn that a user tends to prefer fewer changes, earlier completion or
higher liquidity.
But learned preferences should remain distinguishable from mandatory rules.
A suitable hierarchy is:
1. Mandatory constraints and legal or safety requirements.
2. Explicit user decisions and commitments.
3. User-configured objective priorities.
4. Learned preferences, used as suggestions.
5. Algorithm defaults.
The optimizer must not silently weaken a hard constraint because a model predicts that the user
might prefer a different outcome.
6. Generalize the AI layer into a planning copilot
The AI assistant should be a user-facing interface to the planning engine, not the planning engine
itself.
A universal interaction should work like this:
The AI should be able to ask domain-appropriate questions rather than force everyone through a

For instance:
A factory planner may need to specify routings, machine capacity and material availability.
A student may need to specify available hours, syllabus, examination date and revision

9. What makes the product genuinely defensible?
The architecture alone is not a moat. Python, React, GA, CP-SAT and LLM integration are available to
competitors.
A stronger product needs advantages that accumulate through usage.
1. Reusable problem modeling
A user can express a new class of planning problem without rebuilding the entire application.
Domain adapters can be assembled from reusable model components.
2. Algorithm portfolio and benchmarking
The platform learns which solver strategies work well for particular problem structures, using
reproducible benchmarks and actual outcome data. Solver selection remains explainable and
measurable.
3. Trustworthy constraint enforcement
Independent validation, conflict explanations, versioned assumptions and honest uncertainty
reporting make plans reliable enough for real operations.
4. Closed-loop replanning
The platform learns from actual execution: delays, changed availability, realized costs and
completed work. It can improve future planning without silently changing user policy.
5. Natural-language-to-executable-plan workflow
Users can go from a description to a structured model, validated candidate plans,
explanations and execution tracking without needing to learn mathematical programming.
The strongest combination would be a low-friction modeling experience plus proven optimization
and reliable execution. That is much harder to replicate than a polished dashboard or a generic AI
chat interface.

A universal architecture does not require a universal launch.

competitors and regulatory requirements.
I would build the shared core but launch through one well-defined market.

11. A practical competitive-validation plan
Before committing months to implementation, test the product hypothesis against existing tools.
1. Study Timefold deeply. Examine its modeling approach, constraint semantics, solver capabilities,
API model and real-time replanning. Identify what you would reuse, what you would build and
what customers still have to implement themselves. Timefold Doc… +2
2. Benchmark against a domain specialist. For manufacturing, compare a representative problem
against a relevant APS product or a strong mathematical optimization baseline. Measure
feasibility, runtime, plan quality and replanning disruption.
3. Interview 10–15 potential users in one target segment. Ask how they plan today, which
constraints spreadsheets cannot handle, how often plans break, and what a better plan is worth
financially or operationally.
4. Build a thin vertical prototype. Let a user describe a problem, correct the structured model,
generate alternatives, inspect constraints and approve a plan.
5. Test willingness to pay. Seek pilot users who will provide representative data and evaluate
measurable improvements, not merely people who like the idea.
The critical questions are:
Is the current planning process genuinely painful?
Are existing products too expensive, too complex or too domain-specific for the intended
customer?
Does the new platform improve outcomes, not just automate data entry?
Can the product be configured for a second domain without rewriting the engine?
Is there a buyer who will pay for the result?

Implement a second, mathematically different domain and measure how much of the core
can be reused without introducing domain-specific hacks.
05 Expand into a platform
Based on evidence
Add reusable domain templates, natural-language model creation, solver benchmarking,
scenario simulation, live replanning and eventually additional commercial domains.
These estimates are planning ranges, not commitments. The actual timeline depends on team size,
solver complexity, integrations and how much of the first domain can be reused.
Final verdict
I believe the idea is technically feasible and commercially worth investigating, but the opportunity is
not simply “a universal Genetic Algorithm.” General-purpose optimization engines already exist, and
specialized planning applications are mature.
The more compelling product is:
A universal planning interface backed by a rigorous optimization engine, configurable domain
models, trustworthy validation, explainable trade-offs and continuous replanning.
Build the core for generality. Enter the market through one problem where you can demonstrate
measurable value. Expand only after proving that the architecture genuinely transfers across domains.
The first competitor I would study is Timefold. The first question I would answer is not whether you
can build another solver. It is whether you can make complex optimization dramatically easier to
model, use, trust and adapt than the alternatives.
